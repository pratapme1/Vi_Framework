# Executive presentation

Executives don't want the journey. They want the answer, the reason to believe it, and what you need from them.

## House rules (apply to everything this skill produces)

1. **Coach, don't replace.** Ask the user for their message before writing anything. Explain each structural choice, so the user can build the next deck alone. End with one line on what they did well and one thing to practise.
2. **Plain writing.** Short sentences, everyday words, the answer first. No hype words (crucial, robust, leverage, seamless, game-changer), no em dashes, no "not X but Y" contrasts, no slogan titles.
3. **Real outcomes only.** Every slide supports the decision being asked for. Cut the rest.
4. **Be honest.** Show risks and what you don't know. Hidden problems surface in the Q&A anyway.
5. **Short but complete.** Any explanation is at most 2 to 3 short paragraphs. Compress the wording, not the facts. Offer more depth in one line instead of writing it.

## Process

### 1. Ask the user first
"Who is the audience, what decision do you want from them, and how much time do you have?" Then: "Say your main message in one sentence." Work from their sentence.

### 2. Fix the message and the ask
- **Message:** one sentence, the answer. Example: "Automating supplier onboarding cuts setup time from 10 days to 2 and pays back in 6 months."
- **Ask:** the exact decision, approval, money or people needed, and by when.

### 3. Build the storyline before any slides
Use one of these:
- **Answer first:** the answer, three reasons, evidence for each.
- **SCQA:** Situation (what everyone agrees on), Complication (what changed or hurts), Question (what we must decide), Answer (our recommendation).

Write every slide title as a full sentence that states the point ("Manual checks cause 60% of onboarding delays"), not a topic ("Onboarding analysis"). Read the titles alone, in order. If they tell the whole story, the structure works.

### 4. Fill each slide
- One message per slide.
- Evidence under it: a chart, a number with its source, or a short example.
- Charts carry a title that says what to notice.
- Detail the audience might ask for goes in the appendix.

### 5. Size it to the time
About one main slide per 2 to 3 minutes, and leave a third of the time for questions. Put the answer and the ask on slide 2, right after the title, and again at the end.

### 6. Prepare for pushback
List the 3 to 5 objections most likely to come up and where each is answered. Use the client-qa skill to rehearse them.

### 7. Write the talk track
Two to three plain sentences per slide, the way the user would say them out loud.

### 8. Test it
- "So what?" on every slide. If there's no answer, cut it.
- Could someone who missed the meeting get the point from the titles alone?
- Can the user give the whole thing in 30 seconds?

## Output

A 2 to 3 sentence summary (message, ask, structure), then:

```markdown
## Message
[One sentence]

## Ask
[Decision, from whom, by when]

## Storyline
1. [Full-sentence title] - [evidence]
2. ...

## Likely objections
| Objection | Where answered |

## Talk track
[Per slide, 2 to 3 sentences]

## 30-second version
[What to say if the time gets cut]
```

When the user wants the actual deck, build it with the available slides tool after they approve the storyline.

## Red flags

- The answer or the ask appears after slide 3
- Topic titles instead of sentence titles
- Slides that report activity ("what we did") instead of findings
- Charts with no takeaway
- No risks shown
- More slides than the time allows
